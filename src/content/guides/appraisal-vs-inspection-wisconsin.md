These two happen within a couple of weeks of each other, both involve a stranger walking through the house with a clipboard, and both produce a report that can blow up your deal. So buyers mix them up constantly.

They are not the same thing, they are not for the same person, and confusing them can cost you real money.

## The short version

**An appraisal answers one question: is this house worth what the lender is about to lend against it?** It exists to protect the lender. You pay for it, but it is not for you.

**An inspection answers a different question: what is actually wrong with this house?** It exists to protect you. It is optional, and skipping it is one of the more expensive mistakes a buyer can make.

## The appraisal

Your lender orders it once you are under contract. A licensed appraiser looks at the home and compares it to recent nearby sales of similar properties, then produces an opinion of value ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-an-appraisal-en-1943/)).

What matters about it:

- **You usually pay for it**, typically as part of your closing costs, even though the report belongs to the lender.
- **It is about value, not condition.** An appraiser notes obvious problems, particularly anything affecting safety or habitability, but is not crawling through your attic looking for issues.
- **It uses comparable sales, not asking prices.** This is why the median list price on a market data page is a starting point and not an appraisal. Comparable sold homes are what count.

### What happens if it comes in low

This is the scenario buyers actually need to plan for. If the appraisal lands below your agreed price, your lender will only lend against the lower figure, which opens a gap.

Your realistic options are to make up the difference in cash, renegotiate the price with the seller, challenge the appraisal if there is a factual error or a genuinely better comparable, or walk away if your offer protected you. Which of those you can actually use depends on how your offer was written, which is a conversation to have with your agent **before** you write it, not after the number comes back.

## The inspection

You order this one, and you choose who does it. A home inspector examines the physical condition of the property and reports what they find.

In Wisconsin, home inspectors are licensed by the Department of Safety and Professional Services, and you can verify a licence before you hire someone ([Wisconsin DSPS](https://dsps.wi.gov/pages/Professions/HomeInspector/Default.aspx)).

What matters about it:

- **It is your report.** You paid for it, it belongs to you, and the lender never sees it.
- **It is about condition, not value.** An inspector will not tell you whether you are overpaying.
- **Go along if you can.** An hour walking the house with your inspector teaches you more than the report will, and you get to ask what is urgent and what can wait.

Our [Wisconsin home inspection checklist](/guides/wisconsin-home-inspection-checklist/) covers the local trouble spots worth paying attention to here specifically, including basement moisture, radon, and older sewer laterals.

## Why buyers get this wrong, expensively

Two mistakes come up again and again.

**Assuming the appraisal means the house is fine.** It does not. A home can appraise at full value and still need a $14,000 roof. The appraiser was answering a question about price, not about your furnace.

**Waiving the inspection to win a bid.** In a competitive market this gets suggested a lot, and it is the single riskiest thing a buyer can do. There are less dangerous ways to strengthen an offer, which we cover in our guide to [making a competitive offer](/guides/competitive-home-offer-milwaukee/). If you are considering it at all, do it with your eyes open and with an agent who has told you plainly what you are giving up.

## What to do with both reports

Take them together. The inspection tells you what the house needs; the appraisal tells you what a lender thinks it is worth. A house that appraises at your offer price but needs $30,000 of work is a different purchase from one that appraises the same and needs nothing.

That combination, condition against value, is where a good buyer agent earns their keep: knowing which findings are normal for a home of that age in that area, which ones are worth reopening the negotiation over, and which ones should make you walk.

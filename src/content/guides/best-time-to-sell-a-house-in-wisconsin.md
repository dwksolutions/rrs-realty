Almost every seller asks some version of the same question: is now a good time to list, or should I wait for spring? It is usually answered with folklore. We would rather answer it with the actual numbers.

We track 49 Southeast Wisconsin markets and hold monthly listing data going back to July 2016, which is 120 months across the region. That is enough to see whether the seasons really do behave differently here, or whether it just feels that way.

They do. And the pattern is remarkably consistent.

## What the data shows

Here is the median number of days that homes on the market had been listed, averaged by calendar month across the full ten years, alongside how many homes were listed in a typical market.

| Month | Days on market | Listings per market |
| --- | --- | --- |
| January | 61 | 42 |
| February | 45 | 42 |
| March | 36 | 47 |
| April | 34 | 53 |
| May | **32** | 60 |
| June | 33 | 67 |
| July | 36 | **73** |
| August | 37 | 73 |
| September | 39 | 73 |
| October | 42 | 70 |
| November | 46 | 62 |
| December | 57 | 50 |

Two things stand out. Inventory in January has been sitting roughly **twice as long** as inventory in May, 61 days against 32. And the number of homes for sale nearly doubles between February and July.

## The pattern held every single year

A ten-year average can hide a lot. One wild year can create a trend that never really existed, so we checked each year separately.

January inventory was slower-moving than May inventory in **10 out of 10 years**, without a single exception, through a housing boom, a pandemic, and the rate rises that followed:

| Year | January | May |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2017 | 88 days | 41 days |
| 2018 | 76 | 37 |
| 2019 | 69 | 39 |
| 2020 | 69 | 43 |
| 2021 | 49 | 26 |
| 2022 | 55 | 24 |
| 2023 | 56 | 29 |
| 2024 | 52 | 29 |
| 2025 | 51 | 29 |
| 2026 | 56 | 30 |

The gap narrows in hot years and widens in slow ones, but it never closes or reverses. Whatever else changes in this market, the seasons keep behaving the same way.

## What this actually measures, and what it does not

This is the part most articles skip, and it changes how you should read the table above.

The figure is **how long the homes currently for sale have been sitting**, not how long a home listed in that month took to sell. Those are different things, and the difference matters in winter especially.

January inventory is largely carryover. It is the homes that were listed in September and October and did not sell, still sitting there while few new listings arrive to refresh the pool. So a high January number partly reflects a picked-over market rather than a prediction about your house.

What you can fairly take from it:

- **Spring inventory turns over fastest.** Homes are not lingering, which means buyers are active and absorbing what comes to market.
- **Winter is thin on both sides.** Fewer buyers looking, and fewer sellers competing with you.
- **Summer has the most competition.** July through September carry the most listings of any point in the year.

What you cannot fairly take from it is a promise. "List in May and sell in 32 days" is not what this data says, and any agent who tells you otherwise is reading it wrong.

## So when should you list?

The honest answer is that the season matters less than the four things you control.

**Your pricing.** A well-priced home in January outperforms an overpriced one in May, and it is not close. Seasonality moves the number of buyers looking at your home. Price moves whether they make an offer. Our guide to [what your home is actually worth](/guides/whats-my-home-worth-southeast-wisconsin/) covers how that number gets set, and why online estimates are a poor place to start.

**Your condition and presentation.** Spring flatters a house. Good photography and genuine preparation flatter it in any month, and matter more.

**Your competition.** This is the argument for listing outside peak season that most sellers never hear. In February the typical market carries 42 listings; in July it carries 73. Fewer buyers are looking in late winter, but yours may be one of very few homes they have to choose from. If your home shows well, less competition can be worth more than more foot traffic.

**Your own timeline.** A forced sale rarely gets top dollar, whatever the month. Flexibility is leverage, and if waiting three months means you are not negotiating under pressure, that is usually worth more than any seasonal edge. If you are buying as well as selling, [sequencing the two](/guides/buying-and-selling-at-the-same-time-wisconsin/) will drive your timing far more than the season will.

## The local caveat

Everything above is regional. Southeast Wisconsin is not one market, and a lake community in Walworth County does not behave like a Milwaukee neighbourhood. Waterfront listings cluster into the summer months in a way that inland markets do not, which is why some markets swing far more than the regional average.

You can see the current figures for your own city on our [market data pages](/home-values/), or [compare every market we track side by side](/home-values/compare/). For what any of it means for your specific house on your specific street, that is a conversation with someone who works your area.

## The short version

The seasonal pattern in Southeast Wisconsin is real, it is consistent, and it has held for a decade. Spring markets move quickly. Winter markets are slow and thin on both sides. But the pattern describes the market, not your house. Price, condition, and your own timeline will decide your sale far more than the month on the calendar.
